## One supplement I actually tell clients to take

I don't push supplements. Most of the industry is noise — overpriced, overhyped, and unnecessary once your training and nutrition are actually dialed in. I tell clients that before I tell them anything else.

Creatine is the exception. It's the one thing I tell almost every male client to add, and I tell them because the research actually supports it — not because someone is selling it.

If you're a man over 30 who lifts consistently and eats enough protein, creatine monohydrate is one of the few supplements with decades of research behind it. This is what it does, what it doesn't do, and the exact protocol that works.

## What creatine actually does

Creatine is a compound your body makes naturally and stores primarily in muscle tissue as phosphocreatine. When you do a hard set — heavy squats, a sprint, a max-effort deadlift — your muscles burn through ATP (adenosine triphosphate) fast. Phosphocreatine is the emergency fuel source that regenerates ATP during those short, intense bursts.

The more phosphocreatine you have stored, the more you can sustain high-intensity output before you hit the wall. Practically, this means you can push one or two more reps before form breaks down, or recover a little faster between hard sets.

Over time, more reps at higher quality adds up to more training volume, and more training volume done consistently is what drives muscle and strength gains. Creatine doesn't replace the work — it makes the work slightly more productive.

Kreider et al., 2017, the International Society of Sports Nutrition (ISSN) position stand, reviewed the extensive literature regarding creatine supplementation in exercise, sport, and medicine (PMID 28615996). They concluded that creatine monohydrate is the most effective nutritional ergogenic aid available for increasing high-intensity exercise capacity and lean body mass during training. The review notes that creatine supplementation increases intramuscular creatine concentrations, which helps explain the observed performance improvements and greater training adaptations. It also reports potential benefits for post-exercise recovery, injury prevention, thermoregulation, and rehabilitation from injuries while helping athletes tolerate heavy training loads.

## Why your 30s is when it starts to matter more

As you move through your 30s and 40s, training hard while juggling work, family, and slower recovery becomes the main challenge. You cannot afford wasted sets or stalled progress.

For men under 50 who lift, the performance data is clear. In a systematic review and meta-analysis, Wang and colleagues (2024) evaluated the effects of creatine supplementation combined with lifting on muscle strength gains in adults aged under 50 (PMID 39519498). In comparison with a placebo, creatine supplementation combined with lifting produced a reliable difference in upper-body strength (weighted mean difference of 4.43 kg) and lower-body strength (weighted mean difference of 11.35 kg).
 
Wang and colleagues (2024) also conducted subgroup analyses that revealed important findings for male lifters (PMID 39519498). Males who consumed creatine combined with lifting showed reliable strength increases across both upper- and lower-body tests, whereas females in the analyzed trials showed no reliable gains. The researchers also noted a trend for greater upper-body strength improvements for males compared to females, as well as a trend indicating greater lower-body strength gains from higher creatine doses compared to lower doses.

For older adults, Chilibeck et al., 2017 found in a meta-analysis that creatine supplementation combined with resistance training increased lean tissue mass and strength more than resistance training alone (PMID 29138605). Kreider et al., 2017 also highlighted that habitual dietary creatine ingestion (such as 3 g/day) supports performance throughout the lifespan (PMID 28615996). For busy men in their 30s who may not consume large amounts of fresh red meat daily, supplementation can help maintain elevated intramuscular creatine stores.

The upshot: the marginal benefit from supplementing is real, and the window where it matters most is exactly when most men start thinking about it — their 30s and 40s, when training hard but recovering less effortlessly.

## What creatine is not

It is not a steroid. It is not a stimulant. It does not work like pre-workout. You will not feel it hit on day one.

It will not make you lean by itself. Creatine does not burn fat. It increases water retention inside muscle cells, which means the scale may go up 2-3 lbs in the first week. That is muscle cells filling with water, not belly fat. The mirror will not look worse — if anything, muscles will look slightly fuller.

It is also not a shortcut around training consistently and eating enough protein. Creatine added on top of a solid program amplifies results. Creatine added on top of inconsistent training doesn't move the needle.

## The only protocol you need

Creatine monohydrate, 5 grams per day, every day. That's it.

In the ISSN position stand, Kreider and colleagues (2017) noted that habitual low daily ingestion (such as 3 to 5 g/day) is effective for maintaining elevated muscle creatine stores across the lifespan (PMID 28615996). There is no necessity for an aggressive loading phase (20 g/day for a week) — while loading reaches saturation faster, taking a steady 5 g daily achieves full muscle saturation within 3 to 4 weeks without the digestive discomfort or bloating that higher acute doses can cause in some men.

| | Daily 5 g | Loading 20 g/day for a week |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Time to full saturation | 3 to 4 weeks | Faster |
| Digestive discomfort or bloating | No | Possible in some men |
| Necessary | Yes | No |

Take it any time of day — timing doesn't matter nearly as much as consistency. Morning with breakfast, post-workout, at night — pick the habit that sticks and don't overthink it.

Creatine HCl, buffered creatine, and other branded forms cost more and have less research behind them. Plain creatine monohydrate is the form studied in essentially all the major trials. Buy the cheapest unflavored monohydrate you can find from a reputable brand.

## What to pair it with

Creatine works on top of the fundamentals — it doesn't replace them. Protein first: getting to 0.8g per pound of bodyweight daily does more for muscle preservation and growth than creatine ever will. If you're not hitting your protein target, that's the first fix.

Progressive overload in the gym is the other lever creatine amplifies. If your training isn't actually getting harder over time — more weight, more reps, more quality work — creatine cannot compensate for stalled progression.

Think of it this way: creatine helps you get slightly more out of each hard set. The compound effect of slightly more output over months of consistent training is meaningful. But there has to be training and consistent nutrition behind it.

## A note on safety

The research record on creatine safety is extensive. In their position stand, Kreider and colleagues (2017) reviewed safety data across populations ranging from infants to the elderly and confirmed that short- and long-term supplementation of up to 30 g/day for up to 5 years is safe and well-tolerated in healthy individuals (PMID 28615996).

That said, if you have any kidney issues or pre-existing health conditions, check with your doctor before starting. Creatine is filtered through the kidneys, and while there is no evidence it harms healthy kidneys (the same question comes up with protein; see [high-protein diets and kidney health](/science/high-protein-diet-and-kidney-health)), anyone with existing kidney disease or reduced function should get medical clearance first. This is not a blanket concern — it is a specific caveat for a small population.

For healthy men without kidney problems, the safety profile is about as clean as any supplement gets. If you are evaluating concerns around dihydrotestosterone or hair thinning, see our detailed breakdown on [creatine and hair loss](/science/creatine-and-hair-loss).

## The brain research, and how seriously to take it

Creatine's research also extends into brain health, cognitive support, and neurological resilience.

In their comprehensive review, Kreider and colleagues (2017) noted that clinical applications of creatine have been evaluated for concussion and spinal cord neuroprotection, brain and heart ischemia, aging, and neurodegenerative conditions (PMID 28615996). Similarly, in a review on creatine across the lifespan, Smith-Ryan and colleagues (2021) highlighted pre-clinical and clinical evidence indicating positive effects from creatine supplementation on mood and cognition, likely by supporting a pro-energetic environment and restoring brain energy levels and homeostasis (PMID 33800439).

Honest limits: this evidence is earlier-stage than the muscle performance research. Grade it C, not A. There is no separate, settled protocol for cognitive enhancement, and the sensible move for busy men is to stick to the standard 3 to 5 grams daily rather than chasing speculative high-dose protocols.

## Should you take it?

If you're over 30, training consistently 3-4 days a week, hitting your protein, and you want to squeeze more out of each session — yes. The downside is essentially nothing. Five grams a day is cheap, well-researched, and safe.

If you're not training consistently yet, fix that first. Creatine will not rescue a spotty gym schedule. The order of operations matters: build the habit, get the protein, then add creatine as a legitimate performance layer on top.
