## The mistake most men make after they sign up

When men hire an online coach, the natural assumption is that the coach is the variable. Good coach equals good results. Bad coach equals wasted money. That framing is wrong — or at least incomplete.

The coach controls the program design, the adjustments, and the structure. You control whether the data coming in is honest, whether communication is consistent, and whether you actually do the work between check-ins. Most coaching relationships that underperform do so because the client-side behaviors are not in place, not because the coach's programming was wrong.

The men who get the most from coaching treat it as a data partnership. They show up, they track, they communicate, and they do not hide bad weeks. The men who get the least from it outsource their decisions and wait to be told what to do.

## What the science shows about supervision and structured training

The difference between self-directed, unsupervised exercise and structured, supervised training has been evaluated directly in clinical exercise research.

In a 6-month randomized controlled pilot trial, Parmenter and colleagues (2013) compared supervised high-intensity progressive lifting against unsupervised exercise in 22 older adults (mean age 71.1 ± 7.2, mean ankle brachial index 0.55 ± 0.13) with symptomatic peripheral arterial disease (PMID 24219197). Participants were assigned to supervised whole-body high-intensity progressive resistance training (H-PRT, n = 8), low-intensity nonprogressive resistance training (L-RT, n = 7), or an unsupervised walking control group (n = 7).

Exercise adherence was similar across all three groups. However, functional adaptations differed substantially. The supervised high-intensity progressive training group achieved a reliable improvement in total 6-minute walk distance (mean difference: 62.6 ± 58.0 m), whereas the low-intensity group (mean difference: -48.2 ± 67.6 m) and the unsupervised walking group (mean difference: -9.9 ± 52.9 m) failed to improve. The researchers also found that changes in 6-minute walk onset of claudication were independently related to improvements in bilateral calf endurance (correlation coefficient r = 0.65), and changes in 6-minute walk distance were independently related to improvements in bilateral hip extensor endurance (r = 0.71).

The trial by Parmenter and colleagues demonstrates that simply having an exercise routine or performing unsupervised, low-intensity activity does not guarantee physical progress, even when adherence appears high. Without structured progression and external oversight, training stimulus often falls below the threshold required to drive adaptations.

A mixed-methods randomized controlled trial by Glavas and colleagues (2025) explored what occurs when lifters transition from supervised gym-based lifting to unsupervised home-based training (PMID 40731316). The researchers evaluated older adults with obesity across both environments.

Both groups achieved reductions in body mass (supervised gym group: -4.4 ± 0.4 kg; unsupervised home group: -6.2 ± 1.2 kg), with the home group showing greater fat mass loss (mean difference: -3.1 kg, 95% CI: -6.0, -0.3, with a large effect size d = 0.8). However, improvements in physical function occurred only in the supervised gym group.

When Glavas and colleagues analyzed participant perspectives, they identified critical factors for engagement and success: accessibility, accountability, maintaining physical activity levels, motivation, and support from healthcare professionals, along with openness to telehealth videoconferencing for guidance. At the same time, participants navigating unsupervised home workouts experienced clear hurdles, including lack of equipment, limited digital support, and the absence of professional supervision and structured routine. The authors concluded that while home-based exercise can support weight loss, external structure and supervision are critical for improving physical function.

These findings show that unsupervised training frequently lacks the progressive overload, technical precision, and structured routine necessary for long-term physical progress. Online coaching bridges that gap by establishing the structure, accountability, and regular adjustments needed to keep training effective.

## Send data, not updates

The single most important thing you can do in a coaching relationship is show up to check-ins with real numbers — not a narrative.

Your coach does not need to know that you “ate pretty well” or that you “mostly followed the program.” He needs your weight trend across the week, your actual calorie log, your training performance numbers, and how you felt under load. Those four inputs tell him whether the program is working, whether your intake is accurate, and what to adjust.

A vague check-in produces a vague adjustment. A coach who does not know what you actually ate cannot tell you why the scale did not move. Be specific. If you tracked four out of seven days, say so — do not average it and present clean numbers. An honest incomplete data set is more useful than a polished incomplete one.

## Communicate problems as they happen, not at the weekly check-in

Weekly check-ins are for structure and accountability. They are not the right place to tell your coach you have been traveling for ten days, that your shoulder has been bothering you since Tuesday, or that the program has not fit your schedule for the past three weeks.

Those things need to go in as they happen. A good coach adjusts on the fly if he knows what is going on. He cannot do that with information that arrives six days late.

Kris uses iMessage and WhatsApp with no cap on messages. That is not a perk — it is how the coaching actually functions. If something changes mid-week, say it mid-week. “My shoulder felt wrong on the second set of presses today.” That gets a real answer. Waiting until the Friday call to mention it gets a shrug.

## Treat bad weeks as data, not failure

Every man who starts coaching will have a bad week at some point. Work blows up. A birthday weekend goes sideways. A minor injury takes a session off the table. The difference between men who recover and men who quietly disengage is not willpower — it is how they respond on Sunday night.

Bad weeks are data. They tell your coach something specific: whether the plan is not flexible enough for your schedule, whether stress is a recurring variable that needs to be accounted for, or whether one-off events are genuinely random and require no adjustment.

The worst thing you can do after a bad week is go quiet. Coaches can fix a bad week. They cannot fix a client they do not know is struggling until four weeks of silence makes it obvious. Report it. Move on.

## The program changes — that is the point

A lot of men sign up for coaching expecting a plan they follow for twelve weeks. That is not what real coaching is. The plan is a starting point. What happens from week three onward depends entirely on what your data says.

Your calorie target will shift. Your training days might get moved. The cardio might change or disappear. If you are not hitting your protein floor, that gets addressed before anything else. None of this means the original plan was wrong — it means coaching is working.

Resist the urge to follow the original program when reality says to adjust. If Kris moves your training to four days instead of five because the fifth session is consistently not happening, that is not a downgrade. That is a more accurate plan for the life you actually have.

## What to do in the first two weeks

The first two weeks are the most important. They set the data baseline, the communication pattern, and the habit floor that everything else builds on.

In Kris's program, he checks your nutrition and workout logs daily in the first week and gives direct feedback on what he sees. That means your job in week one is simple: log everything, train as scheduled, and respond to what he tells you.

Do not try to get ahead of it. Do not skip logging because the day was rough. The first week is where you prove to yourself and your coach that the structure works in your actual life.

By week two, you know what the check-in rhythm feels like, what the communication cadence looks like, and where the first friction points are. Surface them. The coaches who have the strongest results by month two are the ones who called out the friction points in week two instead of quietly working around them.

## This is not a program — it is a relationship with a feedback loop

The thing that makes coaching different from buying a training app is that someone is looking at your specific data and adjusting based on what they see. That only works if the data is real and the communication is honest.

Men who ghost between check-ins, who clean up their logs before sending them, who wait until something has been broken for three weeks before mentioning it — they get less from coaching than men who treat the relationship as a working partnership. That is not a character judgment. It is just mechanics.

The investment in coaching is not the fee. It is the time you actually show up with honest data over three to six months. The fee buys you access. What you do with it is the return.

## Honest limits

When reviewing the scientific evidence on supervision and structured training, keep several honest limits in mind:

- **Clinical Population Context:** The randomized controlled pilot trial by Parmenter and colleagues (2013, PMID 24219197) evaluated 22 older adults (mean age 71.1 ± 7.2) with symptomatic peripheral arterial disease. While the trial demonstrated that supervised high-intensity progressive lifting (n = 8) produced reliable functional gains in 6-minute walk distance (62.6 ± 58.0 m) compared to unsupervised walking (n = 7) or low-intensity exercise (n = 7), the sample size was small and the specific walking endurance metrics in a clinical population cannot be directly applied to healthy individuals in commercial fitness settings.
- **Lockdown and Home Environment Constraints:** In the mixed-methods trial by Glavas and colleagues (2025, PMID 40731316), participants were older adults with obesity navigating transitions between gym-based and home-based lifting during pandemic lockdowns. While qualitative feedback highlighted the importance of accountability, healthcare professional support, and structured routines, the greater fat mass loss in the home group (-3.1 kg mean difference, effect size d = 0.8) occurred within a dietary weight loss intervention rather than a physique-focused coaching context.
- **Client Communication Is the Bridge:** Remote coaching differs from hands-on laboratory supervision. For an online coach to provide the structure and progression identified in these studies, the client must actively log accurate data and communicate obstacles in real time.
