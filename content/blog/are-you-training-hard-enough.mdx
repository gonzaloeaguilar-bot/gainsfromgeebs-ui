## Most men train to comfort, not to a stimulus

The most common reason a man trains for years without much to show for it isn't his program or his genetics. It's that he isn't training hard enough. Not lazy — he shows up, he does the sessions. The sets just aren't actually hard.

Kris sees it constantly with new clients. They send their training videos in, and the sets are smooth: controlled, comfortable, the last rep moving exactly like the first. It looks like a workout. It mostly isn't one.

Here's the reframe. A set only counts if it was genuinely difficult near the end. A set you finished feeling fine was a rehearsal, not a stimulus. Your body changes in response to a demand it hasn't already met — and comfort is not a demand.

If you are spending four to five hours every week in the gym, doing set after set without challenging your muscles, you are putting in the time without triggering the adaptation. Real progress requires enough mechanical demand to force your body to adapt. When every set stops well short of genuine muscular fatigue, your body has no reason to build new muscle or increase strength.

## The signs you're undertraining

The useful concept here is reps in reserve — RIR — meaning how many more reps you could have done when you racked the weight.

For building muscle, most of your working sets should end with about 1 to 3 reps left in the tank. You stopped — but the next two or three reps would have been a real fight, the kind where the bar slows down and your form has to be held together on purpose.

If you finished a set and could honestly have done another 6, 8, or 10 reps, that set did very little. It sat well inside what your body already handles with ease. Effort is the input that drives the result, and that set had almost none of it.

You don't need a lab to diagnose undertraining. The warning signs show up clearly in your day-to-day training:

- Your working reps never slow down, even on the final rep of your heaviest set.
- Your logbook numbers have not improved in weeks or months.
- You finish every workout feeling like you merely "got a session in" rather than accomplishing focused work.
- You never experience that moment in a set where completing the next repetition requires full concentration and willpower.

If two or more of those are true of your lifting, training intensity is the primary bottleneck holding you back — not your workout split, and not your exercise selection. A stalled logbook and comfortable sets are the same problem viewed from two angles. (Effort is one of four causes of a stall; the others are in [why strength training plateaus](/guides/strength-training-plateau).)

## What the data says about proximity to failure (RIR)

The idea that you need to push close to failure is not just old-school gym lore. It is backed by scientific modeling on proximity to failure and muscular adaptations.

In a comprehensive meta-regression, Robinson and colleagues (2024) explored how estimated proximity to failure relates to both strength gains and muscle hypertrophy (PMID 38970765). The researchers analyzed studies where proximity to failure was quantified as estimated repetitions in reserve (RIR), adjusting for factors such as lifting load, volume equating methods, study duration, and participant training status.

Their findings showed a clear split between strength and muscle growth:

- **For muscle hypertrophy:** In all best-fit models, the marginal slopes for estimated RIR were negative and their confidence intervals did not contain a null point estimate. This indicates that increases in muscle size grew reliably larger as sets were terminated closer to failure (meaning lower RIR).
- **For strength gains:** In all best-fit models for strength, the confidence intervals of the marginal slopes contained a null point estimate, indicating a negligible relationship between estimated RIR and strength gains across a wide range of proximities to failure.

In plain language: you can gain strength across a broader spectrum of effort levels, but when the goal is muscle hypertrophy, leaving too many reps in the tank blunts your results. If you consistently stop sets at 5 or 6 RIR, you are skipping the specific repetitions that produce the greatest muscle-building stimulus.

## So how hard should you train?

The practical target for most lifters is clear: take the vast majority of your working sets to roughly 1 to 3 RIR. On a 10-rep set, that means reps 8, 9, and 10 feel noticeably heavier, the speed of the bar drops visibly, and completing rep 10 requires deep focus, yet your form remains crisp and controlled.

Many lifters attempt to compensate for low training effort by simply adding more sets to their routine. But doing 20 comfortable sets cannot replace 10 challenging sets. To understand why, we have to look at how training volume and effort interact.

## The relationship between effort, volume, and frequency

Weekly set volume is undeniably important, but volume only works when each individual set provides an adequate stimulus.

In a systematic review and meta-analysis, Schoenfeld and colleagues (2017) examined 34 treatment groups from 15 studies to evaluate the dose-response relationship between weekly lifting volume and muscle growth (PMID 27433992). When treating weekly sets as a continuous variable, the researchers found a reliable difference where each additional weekly set per muscle was associated with an increase in effect size of 0.023, equating to an increase in percentage gain of 0.37%.

When comparing lower-volume protocols to higher-volume protocols within individual studies, the higher-volume groups demonstrated an effect size difference of 0.241, which equated to a 3.9% percentage gain difference in muscle size (a reliable difference). When weekly sets were grouped into three categories (<5 sets, 5 to 9 sets, and 10+ sets per muscle group), there was a trend toward greater hypertrophy with 10 or more weekly sets.

However, you cannot scale volume indefinitely to make up for low intensity. A major [meta-regression by Pelland and colleagues (2026)](/science/studies/resistance-training-dose-response-meta-regression-2026) analyzed 67 studies comprising 2,058 participants (79.1% male, 20.9% female; average age 25.16 ± 5.22 years) to map the dose-response relationships of weekly set volume and frequency on muscle hypertrophy and strength (PMID 41343037).

Pelland and colleagues used a fractional quantification method to distinguish between direct sets (counted as 1.0) and indirect sets (counted as 0.5) based on their specificity to the target muscle group. Their key findings included:

1. **Volume and Muscle Growth:** The posterior probability of the marginal slope exceeding zero for volume on both hypertrophy and strength was 100%, confirming that gains in muscle size and strength increase as weekly set volume increases.
2. **Diminishing Returns:** Both best-fit models demonstrated diminishing returns, with the diminishing returns for strength being considerably more pronounced than for hypertrophy.
3. **Training Frequency Differences:** The posterior probability of the marginal slope exceeding zero for weekly frequency's effect on muscle hypertrophy was less than 100%, indicating compatibility with negligible effects. In contrast, the posterior probability for strength was 100%, showing that strength gains increased with higher weekly frequency, though also with diminishing returns.

What does this mean for your workouts? Piling on endless weekly sets or splitting your routine across six days a week will not make up for easy sets. Because weekly volume exhibits diminishing returns and frequency has a negligible independent impact on muscle growth, your priority should be performing moderate weekly volume where every set is executed with honest effort at 1 to 3 RIR.

## The opposite mistake: training too hard

While undertraining is far more common, the opposite error is equally destructive. Some men take every single set to absolute muscular failure, chase extreme muscle soreness as their primary metric of success, and allow their ego to dictate the weight on the bar.

That is not productive intensity — it is recklessness. When you push every set to absolute technical failure:

- Form breaks down, shifting tension away from the target muscle and onto vulnerable joints and connective tissues.
- Systemic fatigue escalates rapidly, making it impossible to maintain performance across subsequent sets and workouts.
- Recovery demands outpace your body's ability to rebuild, leading to stalled progression or overuse injuries.

As demonstrated by Robinson and colleagues (2024), strength gains are comparable across a wide range of estimated RIR (PMID 38970765). This means grinding to absolute failure on heavy compound lifts like squats, deadlifts, and barbell bench presses is unnecessary for building strength and introduces unwarranted injury risk.

Reserve true muscular failure (0 RIR) for safe isolation movements—such as dumbbell lateral raises, bicep curls, or leg extensions—where failing a rep does not compromise safety. On major compound movements, stopping 1 to 2 reps shy of failure provides the full muscle-building stimulus while keeping you healthy for the long haul.

## How to actually train harder

If you realize you have been training in your comfort zone, here is how to recalibrate your intensity without getting injured:

1. **Pick Honest Weights:** If your program calls for 8 to 12 reps and you cruise through 12 reps without slowing down, that load is too light. Increase the weight until rep 11 and 12 require real effort to complete.
2. **Focus on the Final Reps:** A working set does not truly challenge your physiology during the first few comfortable reps. The set begins working when the reps start getting tough. Commit to fighting through those final two to three repetitions with locked-in technique.
3. **Calibrate Your 0 RIR Baseline:** Occasionally take a safe machine or isolation exercise all the way to momentary muscular failure. Experiencing what true 0 RIR feels like gives you an accurate reference point so you can accurately identify 1, 2, and 3 RIR on your other lifts.
4. **Demand Logbook Progression:** If your reps and weights do not increase over time, you are maintaining, not building. Track every set, rep, and weight, and aim to add a rep or a small increment of weight whenever you hit the top of your prescribed rep range.

## Should men over 40 train hard?

Yes, with a plan. A review of 49 studies in 1,328 adults aged 50 and older found weight training added about 1.1 kg of lean body mass, and higher-volume programs gained more. Older people gained a little less (Med Sci Sports Exerc, 2011, PMID 20543750).

A small trial of 28 men and women aged 65 to 78 trained twice a week for 20 weeks. One set and three sets both improved function, and strength and endurance gains were greater with three sets (J Am Geriatr Soc, 2005, PMID 16398892).

So the question is not whether to ease off after 40. It is whether your last sets are hard enough to count.

What this does not prove: both studies are in older adults than most men reading this, and the second trial was small.

## Honest limits

When applying these findings to your own routine, keep these honest limits of the research in mind:

- **Estimated Proximity to Failure:** In the meta-regression by Robinson and colleagues (2024, PMID 38970765), RIR values were estimated from published study descriptions rather than directly recorded by participants in real time. The overall fit of the models was modest, and the exact mathematical dose-response curve between proximity to failure and muscle growth remains an area of ongoing investigation.
- **Participant Demographics:** The meta-regression by Pelland and colleagues (2026, PMID 41343037) drew from 67 studies with participants who were 79.1% male and had an average age of 25.16 ± 5.22 years. Busy professionals managing career stress and limited sleep may experience diminishing returns at lower weekly volumes than younger cohorts.
- **Individual Volume Ceilings:** While Schoenfeld and colleagues (2017, PMID 27433992) demonstrated graded benefits for higher weekly set volumes across 15 studies and 34 treatment groups, individual recovery tolerance varies significantly based on nutrition, [sleep quality](/science/questions/can-pre-workout-at-night-hurt-sleep), and training age.

## Why a coach catches what you can't

Self-evaluating your own training effort is notoriously difficult. Most lifters either overestimate how hard they are pushing (stopping 5 reps before true failure while assuming they were at 1 RIR) or push so recklessly that their technique deteriorates.

This is where having an experienced coach provides an immediate advantage:

- **Objective Video Analysis:** A coach reviews your lift recordings to evaluate bar speed, movement cadence, and technical breakdown, identifying whether a set had 3 RIR left or was truly a working effort.
- **Calibrated Progression:** Instead of guessing weights, a coach structures progressive overload targets that demand weekly improvements without exceeding your recovery capacity.
- **Fatigue Management:** By monitoring your logbook trends and recovery indicators, a coach adjusts volume and intensity before accumulated fatigue turns into a plateau or injury.

Learning to train with genuine, controlled intensity transforms your time in the gym from mindless exercise into purposeful muscle building.

## FAQ

### Why can comfortable workouts stall muscle growth?

Comfortable workouts stall muscle growth because your body only adapts when exposed to demands it has not already mastered. When sets are ended with 5, 6, or more reps left in reserve, the mechanical tension is insufficient to signal the need for new muscle tissue. A set must be genuinely challenging near the end to count as a productive stimulus.

### What are the signs that you're undertraining?

The most common signs of undertraining include working reps that never decelerate, workout logbooks that have not progressed in weeks, finishing sessions without feeling meaningfully challenged, and consistently terminating sets where you could easily complete 6 or more additional repetitions.

### How close to failure should your working sets get?

For muscle hypertrophy, most working sets should end at approximately 1 to 3 repetitions in reserve (RIR). At this intensity, the final reps are noticeably difficult and bar speed slows, but technique remains strict. Heavy compound lifts should stay 1 to 2 reps short of failure, while safe isolation exercises can occasionally be taken to true failure (0 RIR).

### Does adding more sets compensate for training too easily?

No. While weekly volume is important, research by Schoenfeld and colleagues (2017, PMID 27433992) and Pelland and colleagues (2026, PMID 41343037) shows that volume exhibits diminishing returns. Performing extra sets with low effort creates cumulative fatigue without providing the high-threshold stimulus necessary to trigger muscle growth. Quality of effort per set must precede total volume.
